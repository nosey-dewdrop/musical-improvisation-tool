f# or gb? enharmonics, demystified

Press the black key between F and G. What did you play, F sharp or G flat? The honest answer: both. Same pitch, same string, same frequency, two names. Notes like these are called enharmonic equivalents.

Why two names at all?

Because scales are spelled like sentences, and every letter wants to appear exactly once. The F# major scale is written F# G# A# B C# D# E#, one of each letter from A to G. If we wrote Gb instead of F#, the same scale would need to reuse letters and reading it would become a puzzle. So the name is chosen to keep the spelling clean, not because the sound changes.

The famous oddball: E#

Yes, E# is just F. But inside F# major, the 7th note must be some kind of E, because the letter E has not been used yet. Write F and you suddenly have two F letters in one scale. Music notation chose consistency over familiarity, and honestly, it was right.

What this site does about it

The detector compares pitches, not names. Press D# and it will happily match scales that call that note Eb. You never have to think about spelling while playing; the naming only matters when you write music down for someone else to read.
